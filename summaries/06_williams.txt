6. WILLIAMS, K. — GETTING CRITICAL

Reading-list citation: Williams, K. (2022) Getting Critical. 3rd edn. London: Bloomsbury Academic. [Recommended]

PDF actually read: Williams, K. (2022) Getting Critical. 3rd edn. London: Bloomsbury Academic. Pocket Study Skills series. ISBN 978-1-3509-3357-6 (PB); 978-1-3509-3359-0 (ePDF); 978-1-3509-3358-3 (eBook). First published 2009; this edition 2022. Chapter 7 © Catrin Radcliffe. This is the matching edition.

Purpose
A short Pocket Study Skills guide for time-pushed students who have had feedback such as ‘be more critical’, ‘too descriptive’, ‘show more analysis’. Williams shows what ‘being critical’ means in higher education and how to bring that dimension into research, reading and writing. It is not a form of writing (like an essay) and not a hat you put on occasionally; it is a mindset. Everyday criticality — knowing a newspaper is half the story, comparing supermarket offers, trusting one friend’s film recommendation and not another’s — is what you must carry into study. You are not a sponge. Language confuses: ‘critical’ as fault-finding and ‘critical’ as crucial are not what tutors mean.

Part 1: getting a critical mindset
Chapter 1, getting strategic, uses six strategic questions to define the task: what exactly must you produce (format, length, weighting)? What topic? Why (learning outcomes and your own purpose)? Chapter 2, getting critical with assessment, unpacks mark-scheme language. Top-grade criteria across subjects repeatedly reward critical analysis, evaluation and thinking; lower grades are often ‘too descriptive’. A worked grid (knowledge, reading, evidence and analysis, presentation) turns each criterion into questions: What are the key debates? What does ‘wide range’ mean? What is your argument in one sentence? Chapter 3 introduces the stairway to critical thinking (drawn from Open University material, acknowledged pp. 18–19): process (take in information) → describe/understand (key points, assumptions, arguments, evidence) → analyse (how components fit) → compare (similarities and differences) → synthesise (bring sources together to serve your argument) → evaluate (worth, relevance, evidence, relation to other ideas) → apply (use the understanding in assignments) → justify (develop arguments, conclusions, inferences, implications). You start at the bottom. Description is necessary but not sufficient; Level 7 lives on the upper steps. That stairway is the simplest picture of why the PGCE launch rejects name-dropping.

Part 2: research and reading
Chapter 4: a critical approach to reading lists — they are a start, not a cage (your generic list says the same). Chapter 5: a critical search online, beyond Google, especially for open topics such as a dissertation. Chapter 6: ‘But is it any use?’ — evaluating sources. Chapter 7 (Catrin Radcliffe): getting critical with numbers — calculations, charts, whether a figure can be generalised. Chapter 8: to read or not to read, a critical decision (abstracts; Wang, Shi and Zhou is one permissioned extract). Chapter 9: a strategic approach to reading — abstracts, first sentences, report structure (abstract, introduction, conclusions first; methodology questions: was this a sensible approach?). Chapter 10: critical reading and critical appraisal, including annotated bibliography and CASP-style appraisal. ‘Invisible questions’ (p. 38) ask who participants and researchers represent; Black Lives Matter is used to show that shared profiles between researcher and audience can hide whose experience is missing. Permissioned extracts include Tuyisenge and Goldenberg. This is Wallace and Wray’s ‘context’ question in a short guide.

Part 3: writing
Chapter 11: answer the question. Chapter 12: paragraphs as a critical skill. Chapter 13: writing for a critical reader — what first-class feedback actually praises (theoretical concepts, supporting evidence, limitations of techniques, peer-reviewed sources beyond the list, argument stated in the introduction, debates). Argument-essay structure is contrasted pre-university versus university (advice acknowledged to Chara Bakalis on law essays): at university you state your argument, develop reasons paragraph by paragraph, and deal with counter-arguments as you go, rather than a flat for/against list.

Part 4: critical steps
Chapter 14: using frameworks for critical analysis — including amending a framework and justifying the amendment from reading. Chapter 15: stepping up the stairway with illustrations of describe, compare, evaluate, apply. Conclusion: becoming critical. Useful sources and index follow (abstracts, argument, assessment criteria, CASP, evidence, frameworks, and so on).

Implications for SIT4200
Williams is the shortest recommended text and the best ‘first hour’ book. Use the stairway as a self-check: if a paragraph still describes Piaget, you have not climbed. Use the six questions on the assignment brief before you write. Use Chapter 6’s source evaluation on NCCE, EEF and blogs. Use Chapter 13’s feedback language as a description of merit/distinction in the SIT4200 grid. Combine with Wallace and Wray when you need the full postgraduate method.

Climbing the stairway with Computing examples
Process: you take in Rosenshine (2012), the DfE Computing Programme of Study, and the 10X dataset. Describe/understand: Rosenshine’s principles include modelling, guided practice and daily review; the PoS requires programming constructs; 10X includes Tyler (MLD + SLCN) and Ruby (anxiety, LAC). Analyse: modelling is a component that fits first instruction; it may conflict with a discovery-first reading of Papert. Compare: Rosenshine versus Papert versus PRIMM (Sentance and Waite) — similar in wanting pupils to think, different in when the pupil is asked to generate code. Synthesise: you build one argument — explicit models first, then structured making — not three separate ‘theory’ paragraphs. Evaluate: EEF (2021) cautions that cognitive-science labels are not automatic lesson recipes; Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) cautions that a SEND strategy-list is not inclusion. Apply: Lesson 1 is Predict and Run on a selection program, not a blank editor. Justify: therefore the sequence is educationally defensible for mixed-attainment KS3/KS4 Computing, with named adaptations that do not lower the intellectual goal. If you stop at ‘describe’, you are on the bottom steps; the SIT4200 pass/merit line is whether you reach evaluate–apply–justify.

Strategic questions applied to SIT4200
What must you produce? A 4,000-word rationale plus a 4–6 (template: 5) lesson sequence as appendix, EHU Harvard, Computing-specific. What format? Academic rationale with the stated word bands (intro 400–500; theories 800–1000; curriculum 1000–1200; learner needs 800–1000; adaptive teaching 800–1000; conclusion 200–300). Why? Module learning outcomes plus QAA Level 7: critical awareness, original application, judgements with incomplete data. What topic? One from the Computing guidance (Python selection/iteration is the cleanest theory–practice fit). What audience? School-based, as the distinction band of the grid says. What does ‘wide range’ mean in the mark scheme? Seminal plus current, generic plus Computing (NCCE, PoS, PRIMM), not twenty unread items from the generic list. Williams’s point is that these questions, asked before searching, stop uncritical reading.

Evaluating sources in a short-guide way
Chapter 6’s ‘is it any use?’ is the everyday version of Wallace and Wray’s reliability list. For each item ask: Who wrote it? For whom? When? What kind of warrant (trial, guidance, opinion, policy)? Does it transfer to secondary Computing in England? An Ofsted curriculum research review is a professional synthesis with inspection purposes; an EEF toolkit is an evidence synthesis with average effect sizes that may hide subject differences; a blog by a head of Computing may have high ecological validity and low external warrant. Williams would not ban the blog; she would refuse to let it stand alone. Radcliffe’s numbers chapter then asks whether a chart or an effect size is being asked to do more work than it can. You need little statistics in SIT4200, but you do need not to write ‘Hattie proves this works’.

Writing for the marker as a critical reader
Chapter 13’s first-class comments are almost a translation of the SIT4200 distinction column: theoretical concepts used with supporting evidence; limitations of techniques recognised; reading beyond the list; argument stated early; debates identified. The university argument structure (state the line, develop reasons, take on counter-arguments in the same movement) is the paragraph shape of claim–evidence–critique–decision. Pre-university for/against lists produce the weak ‘some say Vygotsky, some say Sweller’ essay that never decides. Williams’s paragraph chapter is where many trainees actually gain marks: one paragraph, one step in the argument, ending in a sequence decision.

Frameworks without idolatry
Chapter 14’s advice on using and amending frameworks is directly relevant if you use TPACK, Bloom, SOLO, or a computational-thinking taxonomy. If you amend it, you must justify the amendment from literature — that is academic evolution, not doodling. If you apply PRIMM as a rigid five-tick lesson plan every day, you have not been critical. Williams’s closing stance is modest: you already make judgements in life; university asks you to make them visible, evidenced, and fair.
