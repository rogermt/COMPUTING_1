2. RIDLEY, D. — THE LITERATURE REVIEW: A STEP-BY-STEP GUIDE FOR STUDENTS

Reading-list citation: Ridley, D. (2019) The Literature Review: A Step-by-Step Guide for Students. 3rd edn. London: Sage. [Essential]

PDF actually read: Ridley, D. (2012) The Literature Review: A Step-by-Step Guide for Students. 2nd edn. London: Sage. First published 2008. This summary follows the 2012 edition. The 2019 edition will have updates; do not invent 2019 page numbers.

Purpose
Ridley teaches students how to find, read, organise, cite and — above all — synthesise literature so that a review is an argument, not a book-by-book summary. That last point is why the reading list calls the book essential: ‘constructing coherent academic arguments rather than summarising sources individually’. The 2012 text uses extracts from real master’s and doctoral work (Ruth Bacigalupo, Emma Coveney, Dawn Culverson, Hannah Fairbrother, Ling Feng, Eileen Lee, Analeen Moore, Morgan Meyer, Dino Ovcina, Key-young Son, Ian Watson, Thomas Webb, among others).

Chapter 1: what a literature review is
A literature review provides background and justification for research (Bruce is quoted to that effect). It looks different at different degree levels (Table 1.1). It may be a dedicated chapter after the introduction, integrated, or both; Ridley shows tables of contents from science and social-science theses (including a second-language master’s and a parallel-computing example). Structuring choices are introduced early: students must choose an approach (Task 1.1) rather than dump everything they have read.

Chapter 2: multiple purposes
A review typically: provides historical background; maps the contemporary context and current debates; sets out theoretical underpinnings; defines terminology; signals a gap in previous research and uses that gap to justify the present study; and shows why the problem matters. Table 2.1 invites you to tick which purposes your own review must serve. Examples include Ling’s discussion of four curriculum perspectives (three of which become her framework) and work on Cold War containment and engagement. Historical sources in the examples include Gaddis (1982) and Platt (1977); childhood sociology examples include James and Prout (1997) and Prout (2005); Parsons (1956) appears in a theoretical discussion. The ‘gap’ is not a complaint that nobody has ever written about your topic; it is a precise claim about what existing work does not yet do, warranting your contribution.

Chapter 3: searching
A literature search is a systematic process with purposes, not a Google habit. Sources include books, journals, theses, official publications, and websites (evaluate them; a site map helps). Different types of research (empirical, theoretical, and so on) sit in different places. Tools include library OPACs, Copac, BUBL, electronic databases and search engines. Because one database does not scan every journal, use more than one. Keywords and Boolean logic (AND, OR, NOT) structure queries. RSS feeds, email alerts and social bookmarking (with caveats about tag clouds and bias) help you keep up to date. MacDougall and Brittain (1998) and Lee (2001) appear among information-science references. Task 3.1 is to track and record searches — a record that later prevents both duplication and accidental plagiarism.

Chapter 4: reading and note-taking
Efficient reading is not passive soaking (see Ridley’s contrast with unquestioning reading). Critical reading asks what the argument is and how strong the evidence is. Speed can be increased without abandoning criticism. Notes exist to force paraphrase, to separate the source’s words from yours, and to record bibliographic detail at the point of reading. Formats include linear notes, pattern notes (Figure 4.1) and tabular comparison of sources (Table 4.1) — a practical way to synthesise. Keywords connect texts; a table makes agreement and disagreement visible. Summary-writing techniques are practised. Hart (1998) and later Machi and McEvoy (2009) sit in the wider ‘how to review’ literature Ridley points to.

Chapter 5: reference management
Three record systems matter: keyword-search records; bibliographic details for the reference list; and a personal library (how you file hard copies). Copyright legislation is not optional: extensive copying of illustrations, maps or diagrams needs permission. Bibliographical software (EndNote is demonstrated; download of details, abstracts and links) is treated as a professional tool, not a gadget. Rudestam and Newton (2007) appear among research-process references.

Chapter 6: structuring the review
Creating a review is a process (Figure 6.1), not a single sitting. Organisational patterns (Figure 6.2) include thematic, chronological, and methodological architectures. Table 6.1 sets out ‘literature review architecture’; Table 6.2 exemplifies it with ‘models of goal-achievement’, showing argument steps (for example, limited previous research on a comparison). The relationship between the thesis introduction and the literature chapter is worked through with real contents pages: the introduction can outline the thesis and the literature chapter can then do the close-up critical work. Signalling language (what has been discussed; what comes next) is illustrated in italics in student extracts. Wellington et al. (2005) is among the research-writing sources.

Chapter 7: in-text citations
References appear in the text and in a list at the end. In-text citations are where propositional content is attributed to another source (Hyland, 2004). Reasons to cite include respect for others’ contributions; allegiance to a research community; credibility; support for assertions; showing how the argument developed; creating a niche by extending or challenging previous studies; comparing and evaluating; proving wide and relevant reading; enabling readers to track sources; allowing accuracy to be checked; and avoiding plagiarism.

Plagiarism is defined as deliberate or unintentional use of others’ ideas, words or work without appropriate acknowledgement. It includes wholesale copying; rephrasing ideas without a reference (unless common knowledge); copying a textbook’s wording even when the idea is common knowledge; acknowledging a source but staying too close to its wording; self-plagiarism (submitting the same wording for two assessments — though a proposal can be adapted into a dissertation); and unreferenced copy-and-paste from the internet. Grey areas include patchworking (Hull and Rose, 1989; Howard, 1995): sentences from several sources, each referenced, but still too close to the originals — and over-dependence on direct quotations, which hides the writer’s interpretation. Turnitin is described as a text-matching tool, not a morality engine.

Referencing systems differ (Table 7.1). Integral citations put the author in the sentence; non-integral citations put the author in parentheses. Disciplines differ in which they prefer (Table 7.2) and in citation type (summary, paraphrase, generalisation, quotation — Table 7.3). Reporting verbs and their tenses carry evaluation (Table 7.4): ‘found’ in the past simple (Chappelle et al., 1995, in an example) is not the same as ‘speculates’ (Wenger, 1998, in an acceptable-summary example). Combined attributions and generalisations are often non-integral. Effective versus unacceptable citations are contrasted, including an acceptable summary of Wenger (1998). Weissberg and Buker (1990) are cited on research-writing conventions.

Chapters 8–9: being critical and writer voice
Critical reading is not yet critical writing. Critical writing compares, contrasts, evaluates, and takes a position. Extracts show how researchers (including Key-young on realism, liberalism and constructivism) adopt a critical approach. Writer voice is how you appear as an organiser of the argument: unattributed assertions followed by supporting citations; explicit connections between citations; summary and evaluation of source material; an overall summary at the end of a section; careful use of personal pronouns (Example 9.7 uses ‘I’ to signpost organisation); choice of citation pattern (Table 9.1 on propositional responsibility and textual voice); evaluative reporting verbs; and evaluative adjectives, adverbs and phrases (Table 9.2 on degrees of commitment). A mixture of strategies is recommended. This is the opposite of a paragraph that is only ‘Author A said… Author B said…’.

Chapter 10: the continuing process
The literature does not end when the literature chapter ends. Discussion chapters use literature to: show findings supporting an existing theory; compare a new model with an existing theory; explain a finding; state a contribution to theory; interpret data; and apply theory to findings (Examples 10.1–10.7). That is how a Computing assignment should use Willingham or Florian in Sections 3–4: not as decoration, but as a tool for interpreting this class and this sequence.

Chapter 11: systematic reviews
A systematic literature review follows an explicit, replicable protocol. Meta-analysis statistically combines quantitative results; narrative synthesis (Popay and colleagues are acknowledged in the front matter) combines studies that cannot be reduced to a single statistic, including ‘vote-counting’ as a weaker option. Appraisal questions (randomisation, blinding, and so on) are rehearsed. An example is discussed; Torgerson, Brooks and Hall (2006) on phonics appears in the references. EPPI-Centre and Cochrane (Higgins and Green, 2011) are further-reading landmarks. Task 11.1 is to find and critique a systematic review in your field. Computing students might later meet EEF evidence reviews in this spirit: a guidance report is not a single guru’s opinion.

Implications for SIT4200
Your 4,000 words are not a literature review chapter, but Ridley’s rules still apply: synthesise; define terms; show a gap (what generic pedagogy does not yet say about Computing); cite with integral/non-integral control; keep writer voice; do not patchwork. Use a comparison table for Sweller versus Vygotsky versus PRIMM before you write Section 1.
