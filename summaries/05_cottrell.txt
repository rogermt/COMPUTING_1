5. COTTRELL, S. — CRITICAL THINKING SKILLS: EFFECTIVE ANALYSIS, ARGUMENT AND REFLECTION

Reading-list citation: Cottrell, S. (2023) Critical Thinking Skills: Effective Analysis, Argument and Reflection. 4th edn. London: Bloomsbury / Red Globe. [Recommended]

PDF actually read: Cottrell, S. (2017) Critical Thinking Skills: Effective analysis, argument and reflection. 3rd edn. London: Red Globe Press / Macmillan International. ISBN 978-1-137-55050-7. Text © Stella Cottrell 2005, 2011, 2017. First published 2005; second edition 2011. This summary follows the 2017 third edition (the scan is split into pages 1–110, 111–220 and 221–332). Do not cite 2023 for page-level claims from 2017.

Purpose
Cottrell’s book is a practical Macmillan Study Skills text for people who have been told they need more critical analysis. It is not an advanced treatise on formal logic; for that she refers readers to Garnham and Oakhill (1994) Thinking and Reasoning, and Fisher (2004) The Logic of Real Arguments. It aims to build the ‘critical muscle’ everyone already uses in everyday life (deciding whether to believe what they hear; checking whether something is likely to be true; arguing their case) so that the same skills meet academic and professional demands. Activities are essential: critical thinking is something you do. Answer pages explain reasons, not only right answers. Companion-website templates support questionnaires and longer texts.

Introduction: why the skills matter
Cottrell argues that critical abilities matter at every age and in every context: noticing what matters; making sound purchases; reading small print; being aware of consequences. Academic study requires increasingly sophisticated analysis of what you hear, see, read, write and of your own practice. Success in most professions requires the same.

Chapters 1–2: what it is, and how well you think
Chapter 1 defines critical thinking, reasoning and argument; why the skills matter; underlying skills and attitudes; self-awareness for accurate judgement; personal strategies; academic contexts; barriers; and the mix of knowledge, skills and attitudes. Priorities for development are set. Chapter 2 is diagnostic: scoring your thinking, focusing attention, identifying difference, recognising sequence, categorising, and close reading. Attention is distinguished from concentration.

Chapters 3–6: finding and testing arguments
Chapter 3, ‘What’s their point?’, captures the author’s position, treats argument as persuasion through reasons, and hunts conclusions. Chapter 4, ‘Is it an argument?’, separates argument from disagreement, description, explanation and summary — the exact fault the PGCE launch names as ‘descriptive only’. Chapter 5, ‘How well do they say it?’, tests clarity of position, internal consistency, logical consistency, independent versus joint reasons, intermediate conclusions used as reasons, summative versus logical conclusions, and logical order. Chapter 6, ‘Reading between the lines’, deals with assumptions, hidden assumptions used as reasons, false premises, implicit arguments, and denoted versus connoted meanings (associations and stereotypes). This is Fisher’s assumption work in workbook form.

Chapters 7–8: flawed reasoning and proof
Chapter 7, ‘Does it add up?’, covers assumed causation, correlations and false correlations, necessary and sufficient conditions, false analogies, deflection, complicity and exclusion, unwarranted leaps and ‘castle of cards’, emotive language and attacking the person, misrepresentation and trivialisation, tautology, and ‘two wrongs don’t make a right’. Chapter 8, ‘Where’s the proof?’, distinguishes primary and secondary sources; literature searches; reputable sources; authenticity, validity, currency and reliability; relevant versus irrelevant evidence; representative samples; probability; sample size and statistical significance; over-generalisation; controlling for variables; fact and opinion; eye-witness testimony; and evaluating a body of evidence. Ennis (1987) ‘A Taxonomy of Critical Thinking Dispositions and Abilities’ is in the bibliography. This chapter is the warrant-and-context step of Wallace and Wray, and the reason you must not treat a US study or a lab cognitive-science paper as automatic proof for an English Computing classroom.

Chapters 9–11: reading, notes, analytical writing, essays
Chapter 9 treats critical reading and note-making: theoretical perspective; relation of theory to argument; categorising and selecting; accurate interpretation; concise notes that analyse an argument or a book; always noting the source. Chapter 10 is critical analytical writing: setting the scene for the reader; writing up a literature search; words that introduce, reinforce and structure a line of reasoning; signposting alternative views; tentative conclusions; academic keywords in titles; introductions; the body as structured argument; citing and referencing (Fisher and Hanstock, 1998, is among citing guides). Chapter 11, ‘Where’s the analysis?’, asks you to evaluate two essays against checklists and then to evaluate your own writing. This is the most direct rehearsal of ‘weak versus strong’ extracts in the PGCE launch.

Chapter 12: critical reflection
Critical reflection is defined and justified. You decide approach and purpose: outcome, focus, model, method, audience, and how experience will relate to theory. A resource outline is offered. Phased reflection is exemplified. Models are compared; Cottrell then presents a Core Model with stages and prompts, including ‘reconstruct’ (each retelling of an event varies). Applications to professional practice and professional judgement follow. A table contrasts good and bad critical reflection (searching versus merely descriptive). Presenting reflection to others is discussed. The bibliography’s reflection cluster includes Borton (1970) Reach, Touch and Teach; Boud, Keogh and Walker (1985) Reflection: Turning Experience into Learning; Driscoll (1994) ‘Reflective Practice for Practice’. Donaldson (1978) and Csikszentmihalyi (1992) appear among wider psychological sources. For SIT4200 Section 4, this chapter is the method: not a diary of placement, but reconstruction, theory, judgement, and what you would change.

Chapter 13: career and employability
The 2017 edition applies critical thinking to career planning, self-evaluation, job search, person specifications, and employer demand for critical thinking (CBI and UUK, 2009; Diamond et al., 2011; British Council skills reports; Chan, Goh and Prest, 2015, on China’s skills gap, among others). Failed applications and scattergun applying are treated as uncritical uses of time. This chapter is not needed for SIT4200 unless you discuss professionalism; the module’s ungraded graduate attributes (reflective skills, creative thinking, complex problem solving) sit in the same family.

Back matter
Texts for activities in Chapters 8, 9 and 11; practice on longer texts; an appendix of search engines and databases; answers; bibliography; index. Bibliography items also include Arnheim (1954/1974); Bodner (1988) on consumer chemistry as critical thinking; Bowlby (1980); Crane (2001); Eco (1998); Farrar (2004a, 2004b, 2004c) as stimulus journalism; Fillion and Arazi (2002) on organic-food claims — Cottrell uses topical claims as exercise material, not as authorities to import into a Computing assignment.

Implications for SIT4200
Use Cottrell as a checklist while drafting: Is this paragraph an argument? Are reasons joint or independent? Is a causal link assumed? Is the sample (or the class dataset) being over-generalised? Is the essay descriptive? Is the reflection a model or an anecdote? The Core Model can structure Section 4. Do not cite Cottrell as if she were a Computing pedagogue; cite her for the skill of analysis, then apply that skill to Sweller, Sentance and Waite, or Florian.

Worked link to the PGCE ‘strong extract’
The launch’s strong Bruner paragraph is a Cottrell Chapter 10 paragraph: it sets the scene (what spiral curriculum claims), uses a reporting verb that is not naive (‘proposes’), signposts an alternative (Fordham, 2016), and draws a tentative, local conclusion (revisit narrative voice through more demanding texts). The weak extract is Cottrell Chapter 4: it is closer to description and summary than to argument. When you write about PRIMM, copy the strong shape: Sentance and Waite propose Predict–Run–Investigate–Modify–Make as a way to manage the load of program comprehension before production; however, PRIMM is not a proof that pupils never need open-ended making, nor that it fits every construct; therefore Lessons 1–3 will stay on Predict–Run–Investigate for selection, and Make will wait until Lesson 4, so that pupils with lower verbal SAS (Tyler Jones; Sarah Barton) meet code as a readable object before they write it.

Reflection versus description in Section 4
Cottrell’s table of good versus poor critical reflection is a mark scheme in plain English. Poor reflection is preoccupied with describing what happened. Good reflection brings a searching eye to a chosen focus, uses a model, relates experience to theory, and produces a judgement that could change practice. The Core Model’s ‘reconstruct’ stage warns you that your memory of a lesson is already an interpretation. For a PGCE written assignment you may be reconstructing a planned sequence rather than a taught one: still use the model. What was intended? What might go wrong for named pupils? What does Florian or the EEF SEND guidance say that challenges your first plan? What will you change in Lesson 5 if the Lesson 2 exit ticket fails? That is reflection with warrant, not a promise to ‘be inclusive’.

Necessary conditions, samples, and the class dataset
Chapter 7’s necessary versus sufficient conditions stop a common assignment error: meeting one condition (a visual handout) is treated as sufficient for access. For Holly Taylor (Y11, VI + SLCN) a high-contrast digital copy may be necessary; it is not sufficient if verbal explanation is still too fast. Chapter 8’s representative samples and over-generalisation stop another error: treating 7X’s SAS table as proof of what ‘Year 7’ needs nationally. The dataset is a planning stimulus. Cottrell would ask: what is the sample, what is it representative of, and what conclusion is unwarranted? Your conclusion should be local: in this hypothetical class, these two barriers will be designed for, and impact will be checked.
