1. WALLACE, M. AND WRAY, A. — CRITICAL READING AND WRITING FOR POSTGRADUATES

Reading-list citation: Wallace, M. and Wray, A. (2026) Critical Reading and Writing for Postgraduates. 5th edn. London: Sage. [Essential]

PDF actually read: Wallace, M. and Wray, A. (2011) Critical Reading and Writing for Postgraduates. 2nd edn. London: Sage. First edition 2006. The 2011 text is the basis of this summary. Core ideas (argument as conclusion plus warranting; critical synopsis and critical analysis; the mental map) are stable across editions, but you must not cite 2026 for claims taken from 2011.

Purpose and audience
Wallace and Wray wrote the book for social-science postgraduates who must engage published (and unpublished) literature in essays, master’s dissertations, doctoral theses, papers and presentations. They also address early-career academics. The book is designed for self-study, research-methods modules, and supervisor–student work. Two ideas structure everything.

First, academic discourse is a two-way, constructively critical process. As a critical reader you evaluate how others try to convince a target audience by developing a sufficiently strong argument. As a self-critical writer you develop your own argument, making it as strong and as clear as possible for your audience (assessors, examiners, journal readers). Successful writers apply the same standards to the literature and to their own commentary on it.

Second, an argument has two major components: a conclusion (claims or assertions about some aspect of the social world, or how to interpret it) and warranting (backing based on evidence). For a conclusion to convince a sceptical audience it must be adequately warranted by sufficient and appropriate evidence. Evidence may include research findings, professional experience, a theoretical definition, or research-ethics guidelines. Incomplete or flawed arguments fail this test. This is the same logic as the PGCE launch’s claim → evidence → critique → decision → impact chain: a decision with no warrant is not Level 7.

Structure of the 2011 edition
Part One (Chapters 1–5) is about becoming a critical reader and self-critical writer. Products at this stage are a critical summary of one text and a comparative critical summary of several. Part Two (Chapters 6–11) develops a mental map for navigating literature, analysing one text in depth, and writing a critical review. Products are a critical review of one text and a comparative critical review. Part Three (Chapters 12–15) puts reviews to work in a dissertation, research papers and oral presentations. Appendices include abridged articles by Wray and Staczek (‘One word or two?’) and Wallace (‘Sharing leadership of schools through teamwork’), a blank critical-analysis form, and a logic checksheet for a dissertation’s overall argument.

Chapter 1: what it means to be critical
The authors distinguish everyday suspicion (scams, hidden motives) from academic criticality. Academic writers are not usually assumed to be lying, but texts still have hidden layers: values, assumptions, and a match (or mismatch) between claims, other authors, and evidence. Critical reading is task-driven: you read for a written task of your own, so your questions decide what to read and how deeply. Self-critical writing is the counterpart: whatever you look for as a reader of others, you should supply as a writer. Table 1.1 helps readers judge where their prior education sits on a continuum from uncritical reproduction to constructively critical engagement. Postgraduate expectations include critical thinking and creativity: making judgements with incomplete data, evaluating methodologies, and communicating conclusions to specialist and non-specialist audiences — language that echoes the QAA Level 7 descriptor.

Chapter 2: making a critical choice
What you choose to read is as important as how you read it. Textbooks, readers, handbooks and encyclopaedias are support literature: they help you into a field but they interpret research for you. Over-reliance shows up as citing ideas without going to the research that produced them. Theoretical literature, data-driven research, practice literature and policy literature generate different kinds of knowledge. Models show how theory and evidence interact (Figure 2.1). Internet material ranges from peer-reviewed journal articles (usually reliable) to unattributed web pages. A claim such as ‘Metaphors are central to how we navigate the world (Lakoff and Johnson)’ is not adequately warranted by a web page; you need the source. March and Olsen’s (1976) ‘garbage can’ metaphor is used later to show how a single image highlights some aspects of organisational decision-making (ambiguity) and hides others (order). Hargreaves (1994), Giddens (1984) and other organisational and sociological sources appear as examples of how concepts travel.

Chapters 3–5: starting to read and write critically
A central question and a review question focus reading. Opinion is distinguished from warranted conclusion. Readers learn to identify the conclusion and the warranting, and to spot incomplete or flawed arguments. Critical Synopsis Questions (for example: what are the authors trying to do in writing this?) produce a short critical synopsis. Self-critical writing begins with a sense of audience (assessors’ characteristics) and with developing an argument rather than a topic tour. A critical summary of one text, then a comparative critical summary of several (examples use Langford, 2000; Butters, 2004; Wray and Staczek, 2005), forces comparison instead of sequential summary. That is Ridley’s point too, and it is what Williams’s stairway calls synthesis.

Chapters 6–8: the mental map
The ‘key’ to the mental map is a set of tools for thinking, used with a warning: writers do not agree on what each tool means. Concepts (for example ‘education’) classify, interpret, describe, explain and evaluate; no one owns a concept’s meaning, so stipulative definitions matter (examples at pages 88 and 111 of the 2011 text). Perspectives are screens made of selected facts, values and assumptions; a cultural perspective (Firestone and Louis, 1999, is cited in the companion Poulson and Wallace volume) highlights shared beliefs and rituals (a degree ceremony as symbol) and downplays other features. Metaphors describe one thing as another; they can be used interchangeably with ‘perspective’ or more narrowly as a single image. Theories and models are further tools. Assumptions and ideologies often remain implicit; Wallace (Appendix 2 / Wallace, 2001) is used to show assumptions under normative theories of educational leadership (Starratt, 1995; Nias et al., 1989; Pounder, 1998; Southworth, 1998; Wallace and Hall, 1994; Grace, 1995; Hall and Southworth, 1997; Lortie, 1969 appear in this cluster).

Knowledge claims vary along two dimensions: certainty and level of abstraction. High-risk writing over-claims; under-ambitious writing never claims enough. Three kinds of knowledge are distinguished: theoretical knowledge, research knowledge, and practice knowledge. Four types of literature attempt to develop them: theoretical, research, practice, and policy. Five sorts of intellectual project lead to literature: knowledge-for-understanding, knowledge-for-critical evaluation, knowledge-for-action, instrumentalism, and reflexive action (the 2011 text sets out characteristics of each). Identifying which project a paper is doing is part of critical analysis. Generalization is treated as a vulnerability: claims that leap from a small sample to a universal rule are easy to reject.

Chapters 9–11: critical analysis and the critical review
Critical Analysis Questions (blank form, Appendix 3) go deeper than synopsis questions. They ask, among other things, how clear the intellectual project is, how claims are warranted, and how tools for thinking are used. Chapter 10 is a worked example on Wallace (2001). Chapter 11 shows how to turn analysis into a written critical review of one text (structure and a ‘perfect’ review) and a comparative review of several. Introducing the text, what the authors were trying to find out, and building the warranting in 150–250 words is modelled. The review is personal in the legitimate sense that you have taken charge of the analysis; it is not a relic of the author’s section headings.

Chapters 12–15: literature reviews, dissertations, papers, talks
A critical literature review is defined and illustrated (including a noun-gender / linguistics example). Structure differs for a single review question and for multiple review questions. Flexible approaches and reviewing multiple themes are discussed. Integrating reviews into a dissertation means matching the study process to the written account, knowing what examiners need, focusing the enquiry, and signposting. Substantive, methodological and theoretical aims are distinguished. The literature should inform empirical work and methods, not sit in a ghetto chapter. A logic checksheet (Appendix 4) tests whether the overall argument of a dissertation is logical (linkage tracker test). Chapter 15 applies the same architecture to research papers (abstract, audience) and oral presentations.

Implications for SIT4200
Do not ‘name-drop’ Vygotsky or Young. State a claim, show its warrant, say how far the warrant transfers to secondary Computing in England, compare it with another source, then take a position that becomes a lesson-sequence decision. That is Wallace and Wray’s method applied to the PGCE chain. Use a critical synopsis when a source is background; use a full critical analysis when a source is doing real work in Section 1 or 2.
