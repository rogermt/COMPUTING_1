3. POULSON, L. AND WALLACE, M. (EDS.) — LEARNING TO READ CRITICALLY IN TEACHING AND LEARNING

Reading-list citation: Poulson, L. and Wallace, M. (eds.) (2004) Learning to Read Critically in Teaching and Learning. London: Sage. [Recommended]

PDF actually read: the same 2004 Sage edition (ISBN 0 7619-4797-3 / 0 7619-4798-1 pbk). This is the matching edition.

Purpose and series
This is a ‘three-in-one’ text in a series edited by Mike Wallace, supporting research-based teaching on master’s and taught doctorate courses. It aims to: develop students’ critical understanding of research literature; increase appreciation of what small-scale investigations can achieve (the scale of a dissertation); and present major findings and concepts in teaching and learning. Professionally conducted research, not previously published in this form, is included so that students can practise critical reviewing on substantial examples. The book is addressed to master’s and doctoral students but is also useful for undergraduates, vocational postgraduates and tutors.

Part 1: becoming a critical consumer
Chapter 1, ‘Critical reading for self-critical writing’ (Wallace and Poulson), is the education-field version of the method later expanded in Wallace and Wray (2011). Being critical means discernment and scepticism, not hostility. Tools for thinking — concepts, perspectives, metaphors, theories, models, assumptions, ideologies — are the key to a mental map. There is no one-to-one correspondence between the social world and people’s interpretations of it: one person’s valuable play-based learning is another’s waste of time. Concepts need stipulative definitions. Perspectives (for example a cultural perspective drawing on Firestone and Louis, 1999) are screens that highlight some facts and values and hide others. Rituals (degree ceremonies, certificates as symbols) illustrate cultural concepts. Metaphors may be broad screens or single images; March and Olsen’s (1976) garbage-can metaphor of organisational decision-making shows how an image captures ambiguity and misses order. Combining perspectives is possible but cognitively costly. Two dimensions of knowledge claims, three kinds of knowledge, four types of literature and five intellectual projects reappear here: this chapter is the parent of Wallace and Wray’s Part Two.

Chapter 2, ‘Designing and writing about research: developing a critical frame of mind’ (Poulson and Wallace), turns the same tools onto the student’s own small-scale study: focus, design, methods, and the written account that examiners will see. Bolam (1999), Gorard (2001), and Miles and Huberman (1994) sit among methodological reference points in the wider volume.

Part 2: research reports (models to practise on)
These chapters are not ‘the assignment reading list’. They are authentic reports on which to practise critical analysis. Use them as Wallace and Wray instruct: identify the intellectual project, the warrant, the tools for thinking, and what is downplayed.

Chapter 3 — Hilda Borko with Kathryn Davinroy, Carribeth Bliem and Kathryn Cumbo: changing instructional and assessment practices through professional development. US context. Useful for asking how far professional-development evidence transfers to an English PGCE placement.

Chapter 4 — Martin Hughes and Pamela Greenhough: learning from homework, a case study (University of Bristol). Small-scale, qualitative, useful as a model of what a modest investigation can warrant — and what it cannot.

Chapter 5 — Gary Fenstermacher and Virginia Richardson: an inquiry into the moral dimensions of teaching (University of Michigan). Raises values and assumptions that a ‘what works’ cognitive-science sequence can skip. Hansen (1993), Green (1999), Sanger (2001) and Chow-Hoy (2001) belong to this moral-philosophical neighbourhood in the volume’s references; Cole (1985) and Cazden (1985) appear in related pedagogical discussion.

Chapter 6 — Harry Torrance and John Pryor: investigating formative classroom assessment. Directly relevant to Black and Wiliam (1998) and Wiliam (2018) on your generic list. Stiggins (1991) is among assessment references. Critique: what does this study warrant for Computing exit tickets?

Chapter 7 — Diane Reay and Madeleine Arnot: participation and control in learning as a pedagogic democratic right? Sociological, political, and about whose voice counts. Useful when the assignment asks about representation, equity and cultural capital, not only SEND labels. Wertsch (1991), Rogoff (1990), and Pollard and Filer (1996) appear in the sociocultural/participation literature around this theme.

Chapter 8 — Louise Poulson and Elias Avramidis: primary teachers’ theoretical beliefs about literacy, an exploratory study. Pajares (1992) on teachers’ beliefs, Duffy and Anderson (1984), and related work on how beliefs shape practice. For Computing, the analogue is teachers’ beliefs about programming (discovery versus explicit instruction; PRIMM versus ‘just let them code’).

Part 3: reporting a literature review
Chapter 9 — Elias Avramidis and Brahm Norwich: promoting inclusive education, a review of literature on teachers’ attitudes towards integration and inclusion. This is the model of a critical literature review in the field, not a research report. It draws on a large attitude literature: Clough and Lindsay (1991); Forlin (1995); Center and Ward (1987); Janney et al. (1995); Scruggs and Mastropieri (1996); Salvia and Munson (1986); Leyser et al. (1994); Weiner (1984); Ford (1985). For SIT4200 Section 4, this chapter is the nearest education-specific demonstration of how to review inclusion research without reducing it to ‘SEND pupils need strategy Y’. Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) on your 2022 reading list is later than this 2004 review; a Level 7 move is to put Avramidis and Norwich (2004) next to Florian and Black-Hawkins (2011) and the EEF (2020) SEND guidance and ask what changed.

Appendices
Appendix 1: useful sources of guidance. Appendix 2: blank form for the critical analysis of a text (the ancestor of Wallace and Wray’s Appendix 3). Author index and general index follow.

How to read the reports critically (the book’s own method)
For each Part 2 chapter, ask: What is the intellectual project (understanding, critique, action)? What is the conclusion and what warrants it? What sample, country, and date limit transfer to an English secondary Computing class in 2026–27? Which tools for thinking (for example ‘formative assessment’, ‘inclusion’, ‘beliefs’) are defined, and which are left implicit? What would a Computing sequence be wrong to copy uncritically?

Implications for SIT4200
The reading list marks this book recommended, not essential, because it is education-specific practice in the Wallace method. You do not need to cite Borko’s professional-development study unless it earns a place. You should use the blank analysis form on one Computing pedagogy paper (for example Sentance and Waite on PRIMM, or Rosenshine, 2012) so that Section 1 is a critical review, not a theory parade. Chapter 9 is the strongest bridge to your inclusion section: attitudes to inclusion are not the same as inclusive pedagogy, and Avramidis and Norwich’s review lets you say so with warrant.

Working a critical analysis of one report (how the book wants you to practise)
Take Torrance and Pryor (Chapter 6) as an example of the method you should apply to Computing sources. First, identify the conclusion: formative classroom assessment is a socially situated practice, not only a set of techniques. Second, identify the warrant: empirical investigation of classroom assessment interactions, not a large randomised trial. Third, name the tools for thinking: ‘formative assessment’ as a concept; possibly a sociocultural perspective on classroom talk. Fourth, ask about the intellectual project: knowledge-for-understanding of classroom assessment, with implications for knowledge-for-action. Fifth, test transfer: a study of (typically primary or mixed) classrooms in a particular national policy moment does not automatically warrant a Year 8 Python lesson’s exit ticket. Sixth, write a position: you will use low-stakes checks (Wiliam, 2018, on your generic list) but you will not claim Torrance and Pryor ‘proved’ that method for Computing. That paragraph is a critical review. A paragraph that only says ‘Torrance and Pryor investigated formative assessment’ is a synopsis, and at Level 7 it is not enough.

The inclusion review as a synthesis model
Avramidis and Norwich do what Ridley demands: they organise by themes in the attitude literature (what teachers believe about integration/inclusion; what variables — for example experience, training, type of need — correlate with those attitudes; what limitations the survey tradition has) rather than by ‘Author A, then Author B’. They draw on Clough and Lindsay (1991), Forlin (1995), Center and Ward (1987), Janney et al. (1995), Scruggs and Mastropieri (1996), Salvia and Munson (1986), Leyser et al. (1994) and others to show patterns and disagreements. For SIT4200 you should not dump this 2004 review into Section 4 as if it were current statutory guidance. You should use it to say that ‘teacher attitude’ research is not the same object as Florian and Black-Hawkins’s (2011) inclusive pedagogy, and that your adaptations will start from the demands of this Computing sequence (PRIMM steps, vocabulary load, public debugging) rather than from a generic attitude to SEND.

Homework, morals, and beliefs — what to steal and what to leave
Hughes and Greenhough’s homework case study models modest, well-bounded claims: a case can warrant a rich interpretation of one setting; it cannot warrant a national policy. Fenstermacher and Richardson force a question the cognitive-science cluster on your list can skip: teaching is a moral practice, not only a memory problem. Reay and Arnot force a political question: participation and control in learning as a right, which connects to the assignment’s language of representation, equity and cultural capital. Poulson and Avramidis on literacy beliefs model how to research teachers’ theories-in-use — useful when you critique your own default (for example ‘pupils should discover loops by tinkering’) against evidence. Borko’s professional-development study models instructional and assessment change at scale in a US district: high interest, low direct transfer. In each case the 2004 book’s value is the practice of critique, not a bank of Computing facts.
